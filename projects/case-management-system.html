Case Management System

Exploring how software systems can manage person identities when records contain incomplete, inconsistent, or potentially duplicated information.

The Problem

In a case management system, the same individual may appear across multiple cases and records. However, names and personal information are not always consistent for a variety of different reasons.

A person might be recorded as "John Doe" in one case and "Jon Doe" in another due to clerical error, erroneous information given by a defendant. The question is how do we determine that these two records describe the same individual?

Similar names alone are not enough to establish identity. Which causes a pain when cleaning up data for reporting purposes, and auditing. Automatically combining records could result in incorrectly associating individuals with cases.

Design Philosophy

Do not make the database more certain than the evidence allows.

The system is designed to preserve the distinction between information reported by a source and the canonical identity maintained by the application.

Rather than automatically merging similar records, the system retains source information and supports evidence-based identity matching and review.

Domain Modeling

The data model separates several concepts that are often treated as though they represent the same thing.

Canonical Person

Represents an internally recognized individual within the system.

Person Record

Preserves information reported by an individual source, including the name and other details as originally provided.

Identity Matching

Represents potential relationships between records and preserves the decisions made during identity review.

Case Participation

Represents an individual's involvement in a case and the role they hold during a particular period.

System Architecture

The application uses a layered backend architecture to separate HTTP handling, business logic, and database access.

  • Routes: Define available API endpoints.
  • Controllers: Handle incoming requests and responses.
  • Services: Enforce application-level business rules.
  • Repositories: Handle database queries.
  • Database: Persist application data.

Implementation

The project is being developed incrementally, with an emphasis on building and testing the backend functionality.

Database Integration

Frontend Integration

Building a lightweight interface that communicates with the API and allows users to interact with person records.

Engineering Decisions & Challenges

Why not automatically merge similar names?

Case management systems routinely encounter situations where different individuals share similar, or even identical, personal information. Consider two twin brothers, Johnathan Marcus Doe and Johnathan Dwayne Doe. They share the same date of birth and residential address. Both are subsequently entered into the system under the name John Doe for two unrelated juvenile offenses. A matching process that relies heavily on names, dates of birth, or residential addresses could incorrectly associate these records with the same individual. Although unique identifiers, such as Social Security or driver's license numbers, can provide stronger evidence of identity, they are not always available or guaranteed to be accurate. For this reason, the system treats matching information as evidence for evaluating potential relationships between records rather than as automatic justification for merging them. Identity associations should be supported by sufficient evidence and, where necessary, human review.

Why separate source records from canonical identities?

Auditability is a primary concern in case management systems. These systems must preserve information as it was originally reported, rather than simply maintaining the most recent version of an individual's information. To support this, the data model distinguishes between a canonical person identity maintained by the application and the individual records that contain information reported by various sources. A canonical person represents the system's recognition of an individual, while source records preserve the information provided about that individual, including names, addresses, and other identifying details. This separation allows the system to maintain a historical record of reported information without overwriting previous values whenever new information becomes available. It also allows multiple source records to be associated with a canonical person without losing the original context in which those records were created.

Why preserve identity review decisions?

Identity resolution is not always straightforward. Unique identifiers may be unavailable, and available information may not provide enough certainty to establish whether two records represent the same individual. As a result, authorized personnel may need to review potential matches and make decisions about whether records should be associated with an existing canonical person. These decisions should never occur without accountability or leave no trace of the reasoning behind them. The system therefore preserves identity review history, including the decision made, the individual responsible for the review, when the decision occurred, and the justification provided. Retaining both positive and negative match decisions provides an audit trail, helps prevent repeated evaluation of previously reviewed matches, and makes identity resolution more transparent and accountable.
